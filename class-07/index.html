The sentence this whole class is about

Not because it forgot. Because nobody asked it on February 28th.

Class 3 solved what. This solves when.

The information has been sitting in the memory for weeks. What is missing is arrival at the moment it matters — and that is a calendar's entire job.

Before the hour · 5 minutes

1
Get the secret URL

Your calendar can hand out a private, read-only address that any program can fetch. Not the sharing link you would send a colleague — the one meant for machines.

Google Calendar: Settings → Settings for my calendars → pick the calendar → Secret address in iCal format.

Apple, Outlook and Fastmail each call it something different and hide it somewhere different — the gate page has all four.

2
Understand what you are holding
That URL is read access to your entire calendar, for anyone who has it

Not scoped to today. Not scoped to titles. Not expiring. Anyone with that string can fetch every event you have, past and future — without logging in as you, and without you ever knowing.

So it goes into an n8n credential and nowhere else. Never a node parameter, never a prompt, never a screenshot, never the class thread.

And that is the trade, made on purpose

In exchange: no OAuth, no consent screen, no Cloud project, no new account — and nothing that can write to your week. Read-only is the right blast radius for step one.

If it leaks, Google's Reset button kills it instantly. One click, no app to unpick.

3
Check it returns a calendar
note the quotes — secret URLs contain characters your shell will eat
curl -s "<your secret URL>" | head -5

You want BEGIN:VCALENDAR. If you get HTML, you copied the browser link.

4
Write down one recurring event

This looks pointless. It is the most important line in the pre-work.

Pick something that repeats — a weekly standup, a monthly invoice — and note the date it next occurs. When P1 lists your day, that is the line you check. Section below explains why.

The two things that will lie to you

Both look like success. Neither errors. This is the segment that earns the hour.

!
Recurring events
0:28 – 0:34

Your weekly standup is not fifty-two events in the feed. It is one event plus a repeat rule (RRULE).

A parser that ignores the rule shows it once — years ago

So today looks quiet. And every one-off event appears perfectly, which is exactly why you will believe it.

Two meetings instead of five, all correct, nothing red. You will plan around it and miss the standup.

how many of yours repeat
curl -s "$BRAIN_ICAL_URL" | grep -c "^RRULE"

That is why you wrote one down. Check that specific event, on that specific date.

!
Timezones and all-day events

DTSTART;VALUE=DATE is a date. DTSTART with a time is a timestamp. And a time can carry a zone, end in Z for UTC, or have no zone at all.

Two different bugs, two different smells

Everything out by the same amount → GENERIC_TIMEZONE is unset, so n8n is on UTC. Class 5 hit this with a 7am digest firing at 2am.

Only some events out → TZID versus floating times. Harder.

P2 — it proposes what you already told it

P2
Propose
0:34 – 0:46

A date inside a document you uploaded weeks ago becomes an .ics in your inbox. One click and it is in your week.

The date is EXTRACTED. Never generated.

It is pulled out of the document text, and the sentence it came from ships with it. A model that invents a plausible renewal date is worse than no reminder, because you will act on it.

If it cannot show you the sentence, it does not ship. That is Class 3's extracted-versus-inferred rule, applied to time.

The UID must be stable

Same document plus same date must produce the same UID every run — otherwise you get a fresh duplicate every week, and you will switch the whole thing off inside a fortnight.

Milestone — in a real mail client

An invite that opens as an invite, dated correctly, carrying the sentence and the filename.

And nothing was in your calendar before you clicked. Check that, deliberately, once.

Why it does not just write to your calendar

★
The argument, said out loud
0:46 – 0:52

A calendar you did not agree to is worse than no calendar.

Give an automation write access to your week and it can quietly put things in it — and you find out when you are somewhere you never agreed to be.

An .ics you click is a proposal. You still say yes.

This is Class 5's draft-don't-send, applied to time — and the same rule follows: moving to write access is a decision to make with evidence, after a month of proposals you actually read.

If you do want it automatic

docs/UPGRADE-write-access.md is honest about the price: a Google consent screen, and tokens that expire after seven days if the OAuth app is left in Testing. If you use Fastmail, iCloud or Nextcloud, CalDAV gives you write access with an app password and none of that.

Where the series ends

The check that proves it was worth building

Something appears in your day that you had forgotten about, and it came out of a document you uploaded weeks ago.

If that never happens, either your documents have no dates in them or you already had perfect recall — and one of those is much more likely than the other.
