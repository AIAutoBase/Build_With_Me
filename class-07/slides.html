A clock it can read

Your brain already knew. Now it tells you on the day it matters. Class 3 taught it to answer what; today it learns when — from your own calendar, and from dates that have been sitting in your documents for weeks.

The line the whole hour turns on

It has known since Class 3 that the insurance policy renews on March 1st. It has never once mentioned that on February 28th.

That is not a memory problem. The information went in weeks ago and it is still there. What is missing is arrival at the moment it matters — and that is a calendar's entire job.

Say this sentence to yourself about your own business before class. A lease, a warranty, an insurance renewal, a filing deadline, a contract with a notice period. Whatever comes to mind is the thing this hour is for — and if you have it in Class 3 already, it is the thing we turn into an invite live in P2.

Before we start

It perceives, remembers, reasons, talks, acts, and can draw its own map — and it still does not know what day it is.

  • Class 1 gave it a terminal it could act in.
  • Class 2 gave it eyes on your inbox.
  • Class 3 gave it memory — documents it can search, and the honesty to say "I don't have that."
  • Class 4 gave it ears and a voice.
  • Class 5 gave it hands and a clock it obeys — a 7am digest that fires whether or not anything happened.
  • Class 6 gave it self-knowledge — a graph of everything you fed it.
The gap, and what closes it todayA schedule trigger is a clock the brain obeys. It knows that it is 7am. It does not know that you have three things on today, or that something you signed in March expires on Friday. Today it gets the other kind of clock — one it reads: your actual commitments.

Stored is not the same as timely.

Two different things are both called "a clock" and it is worth keeping them apart. Class 5's clock makes things happen on a rhythm you set. Today's clock is a source of information — it tells the brain what your week actually contains, which is the one thing a schedule trigger can never know.

Read this before you build anything

This class is designed. It has not been run.

What was measured, and what was notThe reader was built and run on the teaching box on 23 September 2026, against n8n 2.32.6. The parser passes 38 of 38 checks there, and the same checks against a parser that ignores repeat rules pass 23 of 38 — that second number is the one the recurring-events segment is built on. The proposer ran too: it found a real renewal date in a Class 3 document and emailed it as an invite, with no model in the workflow. What was not observed: a mail client opening that invite, and anybody's real calendar. Where a number is missing on these slides, it is missing because nobody took it.
What was verified, and howThe secret iCal URL exists, is read-only and is resettable — checked against Google's own documentation. And the pack's verify.sh feed detection was run, against a real 317-event public feed and a crafted feed containing a recurring event, an all-day event and timezone-carrying events. It found all four correctly.

Running that check found two real bugs in the script, both fixed before it shipped: grep -c prints 0 and exits non-zero, so a || echo 0 fallback produced 0\n0 and every later numeric test died; and the SUMMARY pattern never matched the plain SUMMARY:Title form. Neither would have errored on the day. Both would have quietly mis-reported your feed.

Why you are being told this before anything else: the whole class is about whether you can trust what the brain says about your day. That starts with being told which claims in front of you were tested and which were reasoned.

The habit this series keeps repeating is "designed" versus "run," and this is the first class in a while that is honestly on the "designed" side of it going in. The build happens live, with you. If something takes longer than the run-of-hour says, that is the cost of not having rehearsed it — and it will be said out loud rather than skipped over.

Decided 2026-08-27 · before this hour existed

Four calls, explained

Four questions had to be settled before this class could be outlined: how it reads, how it writes, what it does about the events that repeat, and who is allowed to produce a date. None of them are yours to make today — but you are about to build on top of them, so you deserve to know what was rejected and why.

Being shown the rejected options is worth as much as being shown the chosen one. If you ever wonder "why doesn't it just connect to Google Calendar properly," the next four slides are the answer — with the costs of doing that written down rather than implied.

Decision 1 of 4 · how it reads your calendar

A secret iCal URL. No account, no OAuth, no consent screen.

And it inverts a problem this series already hitContent Mate's calendar work ran into Google refreshing a subscribed feed only every 8 to 24 hours, uncontrollably. That limit does not apply here, because the direction is reversed. We are not asking Google to poll us — we fetch Google. The interval is whatever your n8n schedule says.

Same shape as every "cheap" call in this series: the boring option was chosen on purpose, and the expensive one ships as a documented upgrade rather than being pretended away.

This is the decision that keeps the series' promise. Six classes of "your stack, your data, no rented dependencies" would be an odd thing to end by asking everyone to stand up a Google Cloud project and click through a consent screen for a permission we then deliberately refuse to use.

Decision 2 of 4 · how it puts something in your week

This is Class 5's draft-don't-send, applied to timeClass 5 built drafts it never sends. Today builds invites it never accepts. Same rule, different organ: the automation prepares, you commit.

An .ics you click is a proposal. You still say yes.

There is a version of this class that is more impressive on camera — the brain reads your lease and the event simply appears in your calendar. It is also the version you would switch off within a month, the first time it put something in your week that you would not have agreed to. The full argument gets a slide of its own near the end of the hour, because it is worth saying out loud rather than implying.

Decision 3 of 4 · the events that repeat

We expand the RRULE in the Code node.

OptionVerdict
Expand the RRULE in a Code nodechosen — the real answer
Use an iCal library inside the n8n containereasier, if one is available where you need it. Not guaranteed on every install
Show only non-recurring events, and say sohonest, and disappointing. Rejected as a default

This was open when the class was designed and it is now settled. It is settled because the third option quietly becomes the first one in everybody's head: "it mostly works" is how a calendar you cannot trust survives in your life.

Any of the three is acceptable. Doing the third while implying the first is not.If your own build ends up with recurrence unhandled — a library missing, a rule shape nobody supports — that is a result, not a failure. Write the limitation down somewhere you will see it again. What is not acceptable is a day view that silently omits half your week.

Expanding a recurrence rule properly is genuinely harder than it looks — weekly is easy, "the third Thursday of every month" is not, and then there are exceptions, moved occurrences and rules with an end date. The class builds the common cases correctly and says plainly which shapes it has not covered, rather than implying a completeness nobody has tested.

Decision 4 of 4 · who is allowed to produce a date

The date is extracted. Never generated.

  • Chosen: template the event, and let a model only choose which dates matter. The date itself, and the sentence it came from, are pulled verbatim out of the document.
  • A model may rank candidates and write a readable title.
  • A model may not decide what a plausible renewal date would be.
  • An ambiguous date — "the first of the month," "30 days after signing" — is flagged, not resolved.

Templating is also the reason this class's expected cost is zero. Generating prose would cost a fraction of a cent per event — small, and still not worth it.

A wrong reminder is worse than no reminderAn invented renewal date does not look invented. It arrives in your inbox looking exactly like a real one, you put it in your calendar, you relax — and you miss the actual date. This is Class 3's extracted-versus-inferred rule applied to time, and it is why P2 will not ship a date it cannot show you the sentence for.

Class 3 made refusal a feature: the brain says "I don't have that" instead of producing something plausible. Time is the place where that discipline pays the most, because a date is short, confident and unfalsifiable at a glance — you cannot look at "1 March 2027" and tell whether it was read or imagined.

Part 1 · about five minutes · nothing to sign up for

Setup, at your pace

One URL, which your calendar already generates for you. That is genuinely the whole of the pre-work — but read Step 2 before you copy it, because that URL is more powerful than it looks and the hour will not be the moment to find that out.

1

This is the shortest setup in the series and the one with the sharpest edge in it. Everything else you have installed this series lives behind a login. This is a single string that needs none, and it does not expire.

01

Step 1 · the one thing you need

The address meant for machines — not the one you'd send a colleague.

Google CalendarSettings → Settings for my calendars → pick the calendar → Secret address in iCal format → copy
  • Apple iCloud, Outlook and Fastmail all have an equivalent — each one calls it something different and hides it somewhere different. The paths are in docs/CALENDARS.md.
  • Pick the calendar with your actual commitments in it. If work and personal are separate, take the work one. Start with one, so that when the output looks wrong you know which calendar to compare it against.
If Google does not show you oneSome Workspace policies disable it and some personal accounts do not display it. TROUBLESHOOT.md carries a fallback — and that fallback has not been tested against an account where it is genuinely absent. If that is you, say so in the thread early rather than at 11:14.

Write down which calendar you took it from. You will want that later, when a day looks emptier than you expected.

Two links look almost identical in Google's settings and only one of them is the right one. The public HTML link opens a web page; the secret iCal link returns a text file starting BEGIN:VCALENDAR. Step 3 exists solely so that you find out which one you copied before the hour rather than during it.

02

Step 2 · the only genuinely sharp edge in the class

That string is your whole calendar, to anyone who has it.

Not scoped. Not expiring. Not logged.Not scoped to today. Not scoped to titles. Anyone holding that URL can fetch every event you have — past, present and future — without logging in as you, and without you ever knowing they did.
  • It goes into an n8n credential, and nowhere else.
  • Never into a node parameter, a prompt, a screenshot, or the class thread.
  • Not into a note-taking app you sync, either.
And this is the upside of the designIf it gets out, Google has a Reset button next to the secret address. Click it and the old URL dies instantly. No OAuth app to unpick, no consent to withdraw — and nothing that could write to your week in the first place.

If you post it by accident: reset it, then delete the message. In that order. Deleting first just means you feel better while the URL still works.

The pack's own build script refuses to produce a zip if anything resembling a real secret calendar address reaches it — a Google /private-… path, a webcal:// link with a real host, an iCloud CalDAV endpoint, a published Outlook calendar. It refuses; it does not warn. That guard was tested by planting one, and it caught a false positive on the first run because one of the docs legitimately mentions webcal:// in prose. A guard that blocks its own pack is a guard people delete, so the pattern now requires a real host and a path.

04

Step 4 · the step that seems pointless and is not

Write down one thing that repeats — and the date it next happens.

bring this to the hour · on paper is fineRecurring event: ____________________ Next occurs on: ____________________
  • A weekly standup. A monthly invoice. A Tuesday school run. Something you know repeats and you know when it is next.
  • In the feed, that is stored as one event plus a repeat rule — not one event per week.
  • When P1 lists your day, that is the line you check. Not the whole list. That one line.
Why one line beats the whole listScanning a list for "does this look right" is how you approve a broken parse. Your one-off events will all be there, in the right order, at the right times — so the list looks right. Checking a single event you already know the answer for is a check that can fail, and that is the only kind worth running.

This is the same lesson Class 6 taught with its venv probe, in different clothes: a check that cannot fail is not a check, it is a feeling. "The output looks plausible" cannot fail. "My Tuesday standup appears on Tuesday the 29th" can.

05

Step 5 · "what is on today" is a timezone question first

If n8n is on UTC, "today" is the wrong day for part of every day.

on the brain machinedocker compose exec -T n8n printenv GENERIC_TIMEZONE date
  • Empty output means UTC. Not "probably fine" — UTC.
  • Class 5 already proved this one. A 7am digest fired at 2am, for exactly this reason. Same cause here, different symptom: your evening events show up on the wrong date.
  • n8n reads environment variables when the container starts and never again. Changing it needs a restart.
Two different timezone bugs, and they look differentEverything out by the same amount → GENERIC_TIMEZONE. One fix, one restart.

Only some events out → the harder one. A DTSTART can carry a TZID, or end in Z for UTC, or have neither — a floating time, meaning "local, wherever you are." Three shapes, three behaviours.

Tell the difference before you start changing things. Fixing the easy one when you have the hard one just moves the error.

Worth sitting with: "what is on today" contains a question the computer cannot answer on its own. Today according to whom — the server, the feed, the event, or you? Four answers, and they are only the same answer if somebody made them so.

0:28 · the segment that earns the hour

A weekly standup is one event and a rule — not fifty-two events.

what is actually in the feedBEGIN:VEVENT SUMMARY:Standup DTSTART;TZID=America/New_York:20240108T093000 RRULE:FREQ=WEEKLY;BYDAY=MO END:VEVENT
What a parser that ignores that line shows youThe standup, once — on 8 January 2024. And nothing today. Your one-off events all appear correctly, on the right day, at the right time. Nothing errors. Nothing looks wrong.

Today shows two meetings instead of five. The two that showed up are correct. You will believe it, and you will miss the standup.

The same failure family, for the fifth timeClass 3's base64 stored as text. Class 4's audio file of exactly the right size, full of static. Class 5's digest that only spoke when it had news. Class 6's communities all called Community 1. A plausible artifact from a broken step.

If you remember one thing from this hour a year from now, make it the shape of this failure rather than the syntax of an RRULE. The pattern is: a step breaks, the output still looks like the kind of thing the output is supposed to look like, and nothing anywhere reports an error. Every class in this series has produced one, and they were all found by checking a specific thing the answer was already known for.

0:28 · the other half of that segment

All-day is a date. Timed is a timestamp. They are not the same shape.

three shapes, three behavioursDTSTART;VALUE=DATE:20260923 all-day — a date, no time at all DTSTART;TZID=America/New_York:20260923T140000 timed, with a zone DTSTART:20260923T140000Z timed, UTC DTSTART:20260923T140000 floating — "local, wherever you are"
what your feed actually usescurl -s "$BRAIN_ICAL_URL" | grep -c "TZID="
The symptom, and what it tells youTreat an all-day VALUE=DATE as a timestamp and every all-day event lands at 00:00 and sorts before your early meeting. It is on the right day. It is just pretending to be midnight, and it will keep doing that quietly forever.

The hour check is the timezone check. If everything is out by the same number of hours, that is GENERIC_TIMEZONE. If only some events are out, that is TZID versus floating — and that is the harder bug.

An invoice due date and a 2pm call are different kinds of thing and iCal is right to store them differently. Where it gets painful is that both arrive as a line beginning DTSTART, and code that does not look at the parameters will happily treat one as the other and produce something that sorts, renders and reads perfectly.

P1 · what "it worked" looks like

Your actual day, in time order, with the repeats marked.

readable, not raw JSON09:30 Standup (repeats weekly) 14:00 Call with the insurance broker all day Invoice due — SupplyCo
  • The "repeats weekly" mark is not decoration. It is how you see, at a glance, that recurrence is being expanded at all.
  • Then the reverse test: pick a day with nothing on it. It should say so — not show you yesterday's.
Level 3 · and no amount of green output replaces itOpen your real calendar side by side with the output. Is anything missing? Is anything on the wrong day, or at the wrong hour? Are all-day events reading as all-day rather than as midnight?

This is in VERIFY.md as a level, with checkboxes, because it is the level people skip. Code that looks right is not evidence. Your Tuesday appearing on Tuesday is.

There is a reason the output is a list of lines and not a JSON blob. You are the check here, and you cannot spot a missing meeting in a structure you have to decode first. Making the output readable is not a nicety — it is what makes the verification step possible at all.

P2 · step 4 · two details that decide whether you keep it

A stable UID, and the sentence in the body.

the shape it has to haveBEGIN:VCALENDAR VERSION:2.0 PRODID:... BEGIN:VEVENT UID:stable for this document + date DTSTAMP:when it was generated DTSTART:the reminder date — all-day is usually right SUMMARY:Insurance policy renews (1 Mar) DESCRIPTION:the exact sentence, and the filename END:VEVENT END:VCALENDAR
The UID must be stableSame document, same date → same UID. Otherwise every run creates a second copy of the same reminder, and you will turn the whole thing off inside a fortnight. Then you have nothing.
CRLF, and folded long lines.ics line endings are CRLF and long lines are folded. If a calendar client refuses the file, that is usually why — and the symptom is that the attachment opens as plain text instead of offering to add an event.

The sentence in the DESCRIPTION is what makes this trustworthy in four months. "Because your lease says so, and here is the line" is the difference between accepting it and deleting it.

Both of these are unglamorous and both decide whether the thing survives contact with your actual life. Nobody abandons an automation because it was architecturally impure; they abandon it because it emailed them the same reminder five weeks running, or because the attachment opened as a wall of text on their phone.

P2 · step 6 · the check that proves it is only a proposal

Look at your calendar before you click. It should be empty.

  • Check the calendar first, then click. In that order. That is what proves nothing was added behind you.
  • The attachment offers to add an event — it does not open as text. If it opens as text, the file or the MIME type is wrong.
  • The date is correct, and the date exists in the document. Open the file and find it.
  • The title still makes sense read cold in four months.
  • The description carries the sentence and the filename.
If the date is not in the document — stopSomething is generating rather than extracting, and everything downstream of it is now untrustworthy. Fix that before you trust any of it. A reminder you believe and a fabrication you believe look identical in an inbox.

Nothing was in your calendar before you clicked. That is not a technicality — it is the design.

The order of that first check matters more than it sounds. If you click and then look, you have proved only that clicking works. Looking first and finding an empty slot is the evidence that the automation genuinely has no write access — which is a claim about your setup, not about your intentions, and claims about setups are checkable.

What "it worked" has to mean today

Every check on this list can fail. That is the point.

  • Nothing here fails loudly. A missing RRULE expansion shows you a tidy, correct, incomplete day. A timezone error shows you real events on the wrong date. A generated date arrives looking exactly like an extracted one.
  • So the verification is not "did it run" — it is "what would this print if it were broken, and did I check for that?"
