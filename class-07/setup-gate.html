One URL, and what it grants

About five minutes. No account, no OAuth, no consent screen. But read step 2 before you copy anything.

Nothing here costs anything

Not a free tier — nothing. Your calendar already generates this URL, and an .ics is just a file. The first class in the series where that is literally true.

1
Find the secret URL

Every provider has one. Every provider calls it something different and hides it somewhere different.

What you want in all cases: a private URL ending in .ics whose first line is BEGIN:VCALENDAR. Not the "share with a colleague" link.

Google Calendar

Calendar settings →

Settings for my calendars → click the calendar name → Integrate calendar → Secret address in iCal format.

If it is not there

Real, and it affects a fair number of accounts — Workspace admins can disable it, and some personal accounts simply lack it.

Check you are in that calendar's settings rather than the global ones. Then: make a new calendar with just what you want visible and take its address — often the better answer anyway, since the brain does not need your whole life. docs/CALENDARS.md has the rest. Untested against an account where it is genuinely absent — if that is you, say so in the thread.

Apple iCloud

iCloud.com → Calendar → the share icon → Public Calendar → copy.

It arrives as a webcal address — change the scheme to https for curl and n8n. And "public" means public here, so put only what you need into that calendar.

Outlook / Microsoft 365

Settings → Calendar → Shared calendars → Publish a calendar. You get two links; take the ICS one.

Choose "Can view all details" — the busy-only option gives blocks with no titles, which makes the digest useless.

Fastmail · Nextcloud

Settings → Calendars → Sharing → publish, and copy the ICS URL. Both also speak proper CalDAV, which is the better path if you later want write access without Google's consent screen.

2
Understand what you are holding
This is the only genuinely sharp edge in the class

That URL is read access to your entire calendar, for anyone who has it.

Not scoped to today. Not scoped to titles. Not expiring. Anyone holding the string can fetch every event you have, past and future — without logging in as you, and without you ever knowing they did.

  • It goes into an n8n credential, and nowhere else
  • Never a node parameter, a prompt, a screenshot, or the class thread
  • Not a synced notes app either
And that is the trade, made deliberately

In exchange you get: no OAuth, no consent screen, no Cloud project, no new account — and nothing that can write to your week.

Read-only is the right amount of access for step one, and revoking it is one click: Google has a Reset button beside the secret address; Apple and Outlook unpublish. No app to unpick, no consent to withdraw.

If you ever paste it somewhere public

Reset it. Do not just delete the message. Deleting a post does not un-share a URL that has already been read.

3
Check it returns a calendar
keep the quotes
curl -s "<your secret URL>" | head -5

You want:

what good looks like
BEGIN:VCALENDAR
VERSION:2.0
PRODID:...
What you get insteadWhat it means
HTML, or <!DOCTYPEYou copied the browser link, not the iCal one
401 / 403That is the public address, which needs the calendar to be public. You want the secret one
404The address was reset, or mistyped
Nothing at allQuote the URL. Secret addresses routinely contain characters your shell treats as syntax
4
Write down one recurring event

This step looks pointless. It is the one that catches the class's worst failure.

Your calendar almost certainly has something repeating — a weekly standup, a monthly invoice, a Tuesday school run. In the feed, that is stored as one event plus a repeat rule, not one event per week.

A parser that ignores the rule shows it once — on the day it first happened

Possibly years ago. So today looks quiet.

Every one-off event still appears perfectly. That is exactly why you would believe it, plan around it, and miss the standup.

Saved in this browser only, so it is still here on Wednesday. It never leaves your machine — this page makes no network requests.

When P1 lists your day, that is the line you check.

Curious how many of yours repeat? Once you have the URL in a shell variable:

count them
curl -s "$BRAIN_ICAL_URL" | grep -c "^RRULE"
5
Check the timezone
on the brain machine
docker compose exec -T n8n printenv GENERIC_TIMEZONE
date
"What is on today" is a timezone question first

If GENERIC_TIMEZONE is empty, n8n is on UTC — and "today" is the wrong day for part of every day.

Class 5 hit this exact thing with a 7am digest firing at 2am. n8n reads environment variables when the container starts and never again, so changing it needs a restart.

6
Have a document with a date in it

P2 pulls dates out of your Class 3 documents. A lease, a warranty, an invoice with terms — something with a real date in the text.

The date is extracted, never generated

It comes out of the document, and the sentence it came from ships with it. A model that invents a plausible renewal date is worse than no reminder, because you will act on it.
