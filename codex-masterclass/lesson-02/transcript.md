# Lesson 2: Briefing Astra: goals, context, constraints, planning, and steering

Narrated by Clara Moretti, the AI assistant at Orbix Automation Solutions. Written by Hector Diaz. Northstar Workshops is fictional; all examples are synthetic.

## 01 · Turn a request into a working brief

This is Clara, the AI assistant at Orbix, narrating Hector's class. Lesson two. A request such as “help me launch a workshop” gives the agent a direction, but leaves many decisions open. It might produce a promotional campaign when you needed a teaching outline. It might assume a paid event when you intended a free community session. Better briefing reduces those mismatches before they become a large pile of work.

In this lesson, Northstar Workshops needs a beginner session about turning an idea into a teachable workshop. All business details and outcomes are synthetic. Our goal is a reviewable session plan, not a real launch or a published announcement.

We will build the brief using four fields from the official best-practices guidance: goal, context, constraints, and done when. Then we will practice planning and steering without repeatedly starting over. Think of the brief as a shared working agreement. It should tell the agent what matters, where the evidence comes from, what boundaries apply, and how to know that the assignment has been completed.

## 02 · State the goal as a deliverable

A strong goal describes the result you need to use. For Northstar, “create a thirty-minute beginner session plan” is more actionable than “improve our community.” Add the intended use: the owner will review the plan before teaching a practice session. That prevents the agent from treating public promotion as part of the assignment.

Distinguish the deliverable from the hoped-for business outcome. You may hope that a good session improves participation, but the agent cannot guarantee that result. It can produce an agenda, facilitator notes, and an exercise that you can inspect before teaching.

Avoid locking the goal to unnecessary process details. You do not need to prescribe every sentence or demand a specific number of internal reasoning steps. Define the work product and the decisions that matter. A goal becomes stronger when another person could identify the finished artifact and explain why it is useful. For our example, a structured session plan meets that standard; a general collection of workshop advice does not.

## 03 · Supply context that changes decisions

Useful context changes how the agent approaches the goal. Northstar's participants are creators who have expertise but have never taught a structured workshop. They need plain language, a manageable activity, and a clear example. That information affects pacing and explanation more than a long history of the fictional business.

Provide the facts that should shape the work. Our synthetic session lasts thirty minutes. Learners should leave with one workshop promise and a three-part outline. No specific platform feature is required. The facilitator will teach from notes, so the result should be readable during delivery.

Also name important gaps. We have not specified the number of attendees or whether the session is live or recorded. For an initial draft, the agent can label assumptions or use an activity that works in either format. If a missing fact would materially change the plan, invite a focused question. Context is useful when it supports a decision; length alone does not make a brief more complete or reliable.

## 04 · Write constraints as operating boundaries

Constraints tell the agent which otherwise plausible choices would be wrong for this assignment. For Northstar, the plan must use beginner language, fit thirty minutes, avoid income promises, and remain a draft. These are concrete boundaries because a reviewer can see whether the output follows them.

Some constraints protect fixed facts. If the session is thirty minutes, the agent cannot quietly expand it to ninety because it wants to include more material. Other constraints protect workflow boundaries. Asking for a draft does not include permission to announce it to members.

Keep constraints proportional to the task. A long list of hypothetical restrictions can obscure the few rules that matter. State the critical rules clearly, then add new ones when an actual failure shows a need. If two requirements conflict, ask the agent to identify the conflict and propose a tradeoff. For example, a thirty-minute plan cannot contain six separate ten-minute exercises. Resolving that contradiction is part of briefing, before polished content makes the problem harder to notice.

## 05 · Define done when before drafting

“Done when it looks professional” is difficult to verify. For this assignment, define completion more concretely: the agenda totals thirty minutes, the plan includes one worked example and one learner exercise, every section has facilitator notes, and the final output lists unresolved assumptions.

These criteria create a review path. You can add the durations, locate the exercise, and inspect whether the notes explain how to teach it. They also help the agent stop. Once the required plan and checks are complete, it does not need to create an entire course or a sales page.

Be careful with criteria that measure surface features without measuring usefulness. Requiring five headings is easy to satisfy with empty sections. Pair structure with purpose. The learner exercise should produce a workshop promise and outline, not merely ask learners whether they enjoyed the lesson. Northstar's acceptance standard connects the artifact to an observable learning outcome. That makes the review meaningful even when several different lesson structures could all be reasonable choices.

## 06 · Copy the four-part brief

Read the copyable brief as a complete assignment. It names the output, supplies the fictional learner context, states the boundaries, and describes the checks. You can reuse this structure for a welcome sequence, a research memo, a content calendar, or an analysis report.

For a larger real assignment, expand the context with actual source files and approved examples. Do not assume the agent has seen a document just because it exists somewhere on your computer. Name or attach the relevant material through the capabilities available in your environment.

Notice that the brief does not prescribe a perfect answer. It leaves room for the agent to choose a sensible agenda while requiring a usable result. That balance is important. Too little direction invites guesswork; too much unnecessary detail can make the assignment brittle. After you run the brief, inspect whether the agent's interpretation matches your intent. A short misunderstanding caught now is easier to fix than a complete deliverable built around the wrong audience or purpose.

## 07 · Plan when decisions are expensive

Planning is most useful when a wrong assumption would cause substantial rework. A short welcome draft may need little planning. A session plan with timing, examples, learner activities, and facilitator notes benefits from a brief outline before the agent expands every section.

Ask for a proposed sequence, important assumptions, and the points where your input could change the result. In a client that offers Plan mode, you can use that capability; otherwise, a plain-language request to plan first is enough for this walkthrough. Do not assume every interface uses identical controls.

For Northstar, the plan might allocate five minutes to the promise, eight to an example, twelve to practice, and five to review. That sums to thirty. Before full drafting, decide whether twelve minutes of practice is appropriate for your learners. A useful plan exposes choices at a level you can judge. It should not bury you in dozens of small tasks whose details do not affect the teaching outcome or the acceptance criteria.

## 08 · Inspect an illustrative planned outcome

Here is an illustrative synthetic plan: five minutes to define a workshop promise, eight minutes to study a worked example, twelve minutes to draft a promise and outline, and five minutes to review. The timing adds to thirty, but arithmetic is only the first check.

Examine the learning sequence. Learners receive a concept, see it applied, practice it, and review their work. That order supports the stated outcome. If the practice instead asked them to design a logo, the agenda could still total thirty minutes while failing the assignment.

Now inspect the workload. Beginners need enough instructions to start without spending all twelve minutes guessing what an outline means. The facilitator notes should explain the activity and show a simple structure. This is how planning and acceptance work together: the plan establishes the teaching path, and the criteria test whether the path serves the goal. Treat this agenda as one plausible option, not a benchmark or a reported result from a real Northstar event.

## 09 · Steer without losing the original goal

Suppose the first draft assumes learners already know what a workshop promise is. You do not need to restart the entire assignment. Give a focused steering message: explain the term with a simple before-and-after example, keep the total at thirty minutes, and preserve the learner exercise.

This follow-up tells the agent what changes and what remains binding. Without that guidance, an improvement in one area may create a new failure elsewhere. Adding a long explanation could push the session over time. Simplifying the language could accidentally remove the worked example.

After steering, inspect the affected checks again. Verify the revised timing and confirm that the exercise still produces the intended output. You do not need to repeat every unrelated review step if nothing relevant changed. Northstar's workflow becomes a controlled revision cycle: identify the gap, request a bounded change, and verify the consequences. That is more effective than repeated requests to “try again,” because each turn carries specific information about the result you want.

## 10 · Correct scope drift and contradictions

A common failure is scope drift. The agent adds a registration funnel, promotional emails, and a certificate because those seem helpful for a workshop launch. Yet Northstar requested a session plan. Extra artifacts can consume review time and introduce claims that nobody approved.

Correct the scope directly. Ask the agent to return to the teaching plan and place optional future ideas in a short separate note only if they are useful. Preserve the original acceptance criteria. This keeps enthusiasm from changing the assignment.

A second failure is an unresolved contradiction. Suppose you later request forty minutes of practice while keeping the thirty-minute total. The right response is to surface the conflict, not pretend both requirements fit. Choose whether to extend the session or reduce the practice. You own that business decision. A good brief cannot eliminate every conflict, but it makes conflicts visible. The agent should help you identify a workable tradeoff and then apply it consistently across the final deliverable and its checks.

## 11 · Exercise: brief, plan, and steer

For this exercise, create your own four-part brief for Northstar's thirty-minute beginner workshop. Keep the synthetic audience and intended learner output, but choose a topic you understand well enough to review. That might be planning a cooking demonstration, a photography session, or a simple budgeting workshop without financial recommendations.

Ask for a brief plan first. Check the time total and whether the sequence supports the promised learner output. Then request the full session plan. Identify one real improvement and issue a focused steering message that preserves the original constraints.

Submit your brief, the proposed agenda, the steering message, and the revised plan with acceptance evidence. Explain which requirement changed and which checks you repeated. If you cannot find a genuine flaw, introduce a legitimate new preference, such as using a different worked example, and verify its effects. The purpose is to practice controlled revision. You should be able to explain why the final version is more suitable, rather than simply saying that you prefer its wording.

## 12 · Recap: keep intent visible

The four-part brief keeps your intent visible throughout the task. The goal names the result. Context gives the agent the facts that should shape it. Constraints mark the boundaries. Done-when criteria define what must be true before you accept the work.

Planning helps when decisions are ambiguous or expensive to reverse. Steering helps when you learn something during production. Both work best when the original goal remains explicit. A change to tone should not quietly change the audience, and an added explanation should not silently break the time budget.

Northstar now has more than a generated session plan. It has a traceable assignment, a reviewed structure, and a revision that can be explained. Those pieces make future reuse easier because you know why the final artifact looks the way it does. In the next lesson, we will move durable facts and working rules into files so you do not have to reconstruct them in every prompt.
